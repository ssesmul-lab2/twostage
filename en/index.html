01What is it

What is a two-stage exam?

A two-stage exam is an assessment in which students first complete an exam on their own (stage 1) and then, right away, solve the same exam again in small teams (stage 2). The point isn’t to test twice. The point is to turn the exam itself into another chance to learn.

STAGE 1

Individual exam

개인 평가
  • Students work alone, as in any exam.
  • It secures individual accountability: everyone prepares and answers for their own work, which prevents free-riding.
  • Suggested time: up to 50 minutes (e.g., 55 minutes in a large physics course)
Same exam
STAGE 2

Team exam

팀 평가
  • Small teams solve the same questions again.
  • They explain, challenge and reach consensus on a final team answer.
  • Suggested time: 20–30 minutes (e.g., 30 minutes in a large physics course)
Final score = individual score × 85% + team score × 15%Carl Wieman’s guideline for large physics courses (Wieman et al., 2014) · 70 : 30 in the Korean case

Where does it come from? It grew out of the readiness assurance process of Team-Based Learning (TBL): an individual readiness assurance test (iRAT) followed by the same test taken as a team (tRAT) (Michaelsen et al., 2004).

02Why now

Why two-stage exams, and why now?

Assessment should support learning. Generative AI is shaking up take-home assignments, and the future demands people who can collaborate. These three shifts meet in one place.

Purpose of assessment

Exams used to give no feedback

Traditional exams stop at measuring what individuals can do. Assessment for learning holds that assessment should drive learning. In a two-stage exam, students get feedback from peers right after the test, exactly when they are most receptive to it.

Generative AI

Take-home assessment is losing validity

When ChatGPT arrived, universities began questioning the validity of assignment-based assessment. A two-stage exam assesses people thinking together in the classroom, and researchers are now designing ways to use AI as a tool for collaboration.

Future skills

Collaboration is a core competency

The OECD Education 2030 framework emphasizes student agency and transformative competencies. Korea’s full rollout of the high school credit system (2025) and wider undeclared-major admissions bring students from different backgrounds together. Collaborative problem solving should be built into assessment itself.

04Evidence

What research says about two-stage exams

From physics, chemistry, biology and sociology courses in North America to university courses in Korea, half a century of research points the same way.

↑

Achievement

Team scores are higher than individual scores, and even teams made up only of students who missed a question solved it. Students learned from each other within the team.

↑

Long-term retention

Compared with traditional exams, students who took two-stage exams retained course content longer.

↓

Test anxiety

The group score works as a safety net and discussion with peers is reassuring, so students’ perceived test anxiety dropped substantially.

↑

Motivation & experience

Solving problems together reduced study stress and made learning itself a valuable experience, raising motivation.

Better on the next individual exam

Students who took two-stage exams later scored higher on individual exams.

Bigger gains for those who need it

Effects appeared at all achievement levels, with low-performing students benefiting most, which matters for equity.

Learning, not cheating

Team-stage discussion wasn’t copying answers; it was genuinely solving problems together.

Critical thinking and valuing collaboration

In teacher education courses, students’ critical thinking improved and they came to value collaboration.

We look at the limits, too. In a large introductory biology course, collaborative testing improved immediate performance but did not significantly improve long-term retention (Leight et al., 2012). Two-stage exams may help most in courses that require conceptual reasoning and problem solving rather than rote recall, which is why question design matters.

05How it works

Why do two-stage exams work?

A two-stage exam is not just a retake. Six cognitive and social mechanisms of cooperative learning operate together inside the exam (based on 정혜선, 2019).

Resource sharing

When a friend who knows derivatives meets a friend who knows integrals, problems neither could solve alone get solved.

Constructive activity

Asking, explaining and recalling deepen understanding, especially for the student doing the explaining.

Co-construction of knowledge

Reconciling different interpretations creates new knowledge that no one could build alone.

Mutual monitoring and regulation

Other people’s mistakes are easier to spot than your own. Teammates catch and fix calculation errors.

Social facilitation

People perform better with others. Motivation and engagement rise together.

Technology support

Platforms such as Learning Catalytics and IF-AT scratch cards make automatic scoring and immediate feedback possible.

Vygotsky

Social constructivism

Knowledge first arises between people and is then internalized. Capable peers widen the zone of proximal development (ZPD).

Michaelsen

Team-Based Learning (TBL)

Individual test → team test → appeals → corrective instruction: a structure that turns groups into high-performing learning teams.

06History

Fifty years of evolution: a history of two-stage exams

Rooted in social constructivism and team-based learning, two-stage exams have gone through some 50 years of empirical and methodological research and are now expanding to Asia and to AI. Click a dot or an era to see key studies with links to the originals.

Team-based learning emerged and its potential as assessment was explored. Building on Vygotsky’s social constructivism, Michaelsen’s team-based learning (iRAT → tRAT) provided the prototype of the two-stage exam, and the Johnson brothers’ cooperative learning theory served as its social and psychological engine.

The effects of collaborative testing were validated empirically in many contexts. A paradigm shift took place: exams could go beyond measuring learning (assessment of learning) to promoting it (assessment for learning).

Attention turned to how to implement them well. Guidelines for large STEM courses, balanced team formation, and automatic-scoring and instant-feedback platforms were developed, and research expanded to affective outcomes such as reduced test anxiety.

Research extended to East Asia, known for its competitive exam culture. Two-stage exams were applied with Korean undergraduates for the first time, and a study of how generative AI chatbots affect collaborative problem solving showed the importance of AI access protocols.

08Voices

Voices from Korean classrooms

From interviews with Korean undergraduates and instructors who tried two-stage exams for the first time (Ch. VI of the book, translated).

Challenges found in Korea

  • Teamwork burden: awkwardness with unfamiliar peers, passive teammates, difficulties for introverted students
  • Concerns about face: feeling discouraged when you immediately see that your answer was wrong
  • Time management: running out of time by debating every option
  • Sensitivity to fairness: worry that teammates’ participation affects your grade

Solutions the book proposes

  • Balanced teams: a survey covering year, gender, leadership experience, personality and major (Appendix 1)
  • Orientation and discussion practice: explain the purpose and procedure and build rapport (Appendix 3)
  • Psychological safety: rules for sharing mistakes and three sentences to use together (Appendix 4)
  • Peer evaluation: at least twice a term, with team contribution reflected in grades

09FAQ

Common concerns,
answered

Everyone wonders at first!

QIsn’t a two-stage exam just allowing cheating?

No. The study “Collaborative exams: Cheating? Or learning?” showed that the team stage is genuine team problem solving, not copying, which establishes its legitimacy as assessment (Jang et al., 2017, American Journal of Physics).

QWon’t some students free-ride?

The individual first stage secures each student’s accountability. Add peer evaluation at least twice a term to reflect team contribution, with items such as “nominate the two teammates who contributed most.” Interestingly, as Korean students’ anxiety fell, their sense of responsibility to the team grew, and they prepared harder.

QWon’t team composition make scores unfair?

That’s why balanced team formation is key (Jang & Mazur, 2015). Teams are formed from a survey of gender, year, leadership experience, personality, course history, communication skills and academic background. Keeping the team weight at about 15–30% keeps grades centered on individual scores.

QIs it OK to use easy questions?

If the questions are easy, students just ask “What did you get?”, compare answers and stop. One student said, “I wish the questions gave us something to discuss.” Questions that demand higher-order thinking (analysis, synthesis, evaluation), allow multiple solution paths, and are of moderate-to-high difficulty spark real discussion.

QWon’t there be too little time?

Design the individual exam to take 50 minutes or less, and allow 20–30 minutes for the team stage. First-timers find time allocation hard, so cover discussion methods and time management in an orientation. The book’s appendix includes ready-to-use orientation materials.

QDoes it work for students used to competitive exams, as in Korea?

In a study of Korean undergraduates, students experienced the same benefits as Western students, including motivation and lower test anxiety (Jang et al., 2024). Teamwork burden and concerns about losing face also appeared, so measures that build psychological safety are needed. The book offers concrete tips.

QCan two-stage exams be run online?

Yes, but the instructors interviewed recommended in-person exams, because nonverbal communication matters when several people tackle complex problems in a short time. Collaboration-friendly rooms with round tables or movable desks help.

QCan students use AI chatbots during the team stage?

It depends on the design. Teams with restricted AI use (a shared device, or only at set times) outperformed teams with unlimited use. The more limited the access, the more strategically students discussed what and how to ask (Jang, 2025). AI is a tool and a resource, not “the answer.”

QDoes it work in K–12 schools, not just universities?

Two-stage exams have been used in engineering, the natural sciences, and the humanities and social sciences. A pre-service teacher who experienced one said, “Since it’s about explaining what you studied to others, it seems easy to apply at the middle and high school level.” It also fits assessment reform under Korea’s high school credit system.

QAre “two-stage exam,” “two-stage testing” and “collaborative exam” the same thing?

Yes, they refer to the same format. The research literature calls it a two-stage exam (or examination), two-stage testing, or a two-stage cooperative test, and more broadly classifies it as a form of collaborative (group) testing. In Korean it is called 2단계 평가 or 2단계 시험.
